# Who Actually Owns This Decision

> Two peers — say, an engineering lead and a design lead — each believe they own a decision you also have a stake in, and they've reached different conclusions. There's no standing decision-rights document that settles it. Walk me through how you'd move this forward.

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Influence, Collaboration, And Communication
- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization

## Why This Matters

Most collaboration questions test how a candidate handles disagreement once who-decides is settled. This tests something upstream of that: whether a candidate notices ownership ambiguity itself as the actual problem, rather than just relitigating the substance of the disagreement. A candidate who jumps straight to "here's why my answer is right" has skipped the harder, more senior move — establishing who has standing to decide, and why, before arguing for an outcome. This is a different failure mode than [Overruling the HiPPO](../situational/Overruling-the-HiPPO.md) (a candidate outranked by a decision-maker) or [The Loaded Ask](../situational/The-Loaded-Ask.md) (a request wrapped in someone else's agenda) — here, nobody outranks anyone, and nobody has an agenda beyond genuinely believing they own the call.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- How would you figure out who *should* own this, as opposed to who's asserting ownership loudest?
- What would you do if both peers reasonably believe the decision is theirs, and both are partly right?
- How would you handle it if you needed the decision made this week and a clean resolution wasn't going to happen that fast?
- What would you do differently the next time a similar decision came up, so the ambiguity doesn't repeat?

## Strong Evidence

1. Separates the ownership question from the substantive question — addresses "who decides" before "what's the right answer."
2. Proposes a concrete way to establish or clarify ownership (e.g., naming who's accountable for the outcome, who has the most relevant evidence, or who bears the consequences of getting it wrong) rather than defaulting to seniority or volume.
3. Shows they can operate without a formal decision-rights framework already in place, rather than treating the absence of one as a blocker.
4. Names a way to prevent the same ambiguity from recurring — documenting the decision owner going forward, not just resolving this one instance.

## Warning Signs

1. Jumps straight to arguing for a preferred outcome without ever addressing who should be deciding.
2. Resolves it by deferring to whoever is more senior or more forceful, with no reasoning about actual ownership.
3. Treats the absence of a formal decision-rights framework as an excuse for the decision to stall indefinitely.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Explicitly separates ownership from substance, proposes a defensible way to establish who decides, moves the decision forward under real time pressure, and names a fix to prevent recurrence.
- **3:** Eventually reasons about ownership, but only after being probed, or resolves it in a way that works this once without addressing the recurring pattern.
- **1:** Never distinguishes "who decides" from "what's the right call" — treats this as an ordinary disagreement about substance.
