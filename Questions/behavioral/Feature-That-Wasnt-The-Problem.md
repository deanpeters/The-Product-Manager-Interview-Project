# The Feature That Wasn't the Problem

> Tell me about a time a customer or stakeholder asked for a specific feature, but you concluded that the feature wasn't actually the problem that needed solving. How did you figure that out?

> **Follow-up** An executive brings you an urgent feature request from an important customer and says, "They've already told us exactly what they need." What do you do next?

## Type

Behavioral

## Competencies Assessed

- Customer Understanding And Discovery

## Why This Matters

Taking a feature request at face value is one of the most common ways product teams build the wrong thing. This question tests whether a candidate can separate a stated request from the underlying problem, and the follow-up adds a situational pressure test — can they hold that discipline even when an executive is pushing a specific ask as settled.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What made you suspect the request wasn't the real problem?
- How did you validate what the underlying problem actually was?
- What did you end up building instead, and how did the requester react?
- In the executive scenario, how would you push back without simply saying no?

## Strong Evidence

1. Describes a specific request and the moment or evidence that made them question it, not just a general philosophy about "the five whys."
2. Explains how they validated the underlying problem — a real conversation, data, or observed behavior — rather than asserting it.
3. Describes what they built instead and the outcome, including how the original requester responded.
4. In the follow-up, proposes a way to validate the request without dismissing the customer or the executive's urgency.

## Warning Signs

1. Answer stays theoretical ("you should always ask why") with no specific example.
2. Describes overriding the request based on their own opinion rather than evidence from the customer.
3. In the follow-up, either caves immediately to "just build it" or refuses without offering a constructive path to validate.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Specific example with clear validation evidence, a described outcome, and a thoughtful, non-dismissive answer to the executive scenario.
- **3:** A real example exists but validation is thin, or the executive scenario answer is reasonable but generic.
- **1:** No real example, or the answer reveals a habit of accepting requests at face value.
