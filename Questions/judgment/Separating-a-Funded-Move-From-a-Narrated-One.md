# Separating a Funded Move From a Narrated One

> Tell me about a time a competitor's announcement, or a stakeholder's claim, turned out to be more narrative than reality once you actually checked. How did you check?

> **Follow-up** What's a signal that tells you a competitor is genuinely funding something, versus just saying it?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization
- Ethics, Self-Awareness, And Responsible Decision-Making

## Why This Matters

Press releases, roadmap announcements, and confident stakeholder claims are cheap to make and easy to believe. A PM who reacts to every announcement as if it were already real will chase phantom threats and waste real capacity. This question tests healthy skepticism and whether the candidate has an actual method for verification, not just a gut feel for what to trust.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What made you suspicious enough to check in the first place?
- What did you actually look at — hiring activity, filings, customer reports, your own usage data — versus what you just reasoned about?
- What did you do differently once you knew the real picture?

## Strong Evidence

1. Describes a specific claim they didn't take at face value, with a real reason for the suspicion.
2. Names a concrete verification method — real evidence like hiring patterns, customer conversations, public filings, or direct usage data — not just "I thought about it more."
3. Uses the [evidence ladder](../../docs/evidence-and-pressure-standard.md) implicitly well: distinguishes what they observed directly from what they were told.
4. Describes a real change in behavior or strategy once they knew the actual picture.

## Warning Signs

1. No real verification step — just a restated opinion with more confidence.
2. Treats a stakeholder's or competitor's claim as automatically true or automatically false, with no investigation either way.
3. Nothing changed as a result of the check — the exercise was performative.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Specific claim, genuine suspicion with a real reason, a concrete verification method, and a real behavior change as a result.
- **3:** A real instance of skepticism exists, but the verification method is vague or the resulting change is minor.
- **1:** No real verification — claims were accepted or dismissed on instinct alone.
