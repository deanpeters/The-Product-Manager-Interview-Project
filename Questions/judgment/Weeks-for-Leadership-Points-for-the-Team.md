# Weeks for Leadership, Points for the Team

> An organization sizes epics in calendar weeks for release planning, but sizes the stories underneath those epics in relative points for the team actually doing the work. Why would an organization deliberately choose two different units for two levels of the same hierarchy?

> **Follow-up** What happens six months later if leadership starts treating that epic-level week estimate as a committed date?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization
- Influence, Collaboration, And Communication

## Why This Matters

The choice of unit isn't cosmetic — it's a deliberate organizational-trust decision. Leadership needs something in calendar time to plan releases, marketing, and hiring around. Teams deliberately avoid calendar time at the story level specifically to keep a rough relative guess from calcifying into a promise. This card tests whether a candidate understands that distinction as protective, not arbitrary, and can foresee what breaks when it erodes.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What's actually different about the confidence behind a week estimate versus a point estimate at this scale?
- Whose interests does each unit actually serve?
- If leadership needs a date, what should they legitimately get instead of the raw epic estimate?

## Strong Evidence

1. Explains that calendar weeks serve leadership's real need to plan around a date, while points deliberately stay relative and non-calendar specifically to protect the team from that estimate hardening into a commitment.
2. Recognizes that epic-level estimates are inherently less certain (further out, less broken down) even though they're expressed in the more concrete-sounding unit (weeks).
3. In the follow-up, describes a real, specific failure mode — schedule pressure, forced scope cuts, erosion of trust in future estimates — not just "that would be bad."
4. Offers something constructive leadership should get instead of treating the raw estimate as a date — a range, a confidence level, a checkpoint to re-estimate.

## Warning Signs

1. Treats the two units as interchangeable or arbitrary, with no real reasoning for why they differ.
2. No recognition that the epic-level estimate is actually less certain despite looking more concrete.
3. Follow-up answer is vague about consequences — no specific failure mode named.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Clear reasoning for why the units differ and whom they protect, real recognition of the epic-level estimate's actual uncertainty, and a specific failure mode with a constructive alternative in the follow-up.
- **3:** Some sense that the units serve different purposes, but the reasoning or the follow-up failure mode is generic.
- **1:** No real distinction drawn between the two units or their purposes.
