# Structured Interview Guide

## Purpose

How to assemble and run an interview loop using this repository's question bank, so candidates get a consistent, fair process and interviewers get comparable, scoreable evidence. Pairs with the [competency model](competency-model.md) (what to assess) and the [scoring rubric](scoring-rubric.md) (how to score it).

## Before The Loop

1. **Define the role's priority competencies.** Not every role needs equal depth on all six competencies in the [competency model](competency-model.md). A growth PM role may weight metrics and experimentation more heavily; a platform PM role may weight technical delivery and cross-team influence more heavily. Decide this before selecting questions.
2. **Build a 12-16 question core set**, not a 100-question interview. Cover the priority competencies across the loop; do not try to cover everything in every conversation.
3. **Assign ownership per interviewer.** Each interviewer should own a small, clearly scoped set of competencies for their slot, not attempt to assess the whole candidate alone. Avoid duplicate coverage unless intentionally checking for consistency.
4. **Agree on the same core questions across comparable candidates.** Consistency is what makes scores comparable; ad hoc question selection per candidate undermines the loop.
5. **Select from `Questions/`** by type (behavioral, situational, judgment, method) and competency. Prefer behavioral, situational, and judgment questions; use method/craft questions only where the role specifically calls for that skill. See `AGENTS.md` for what to avoid (brainteasers, trivia, protected characteristics).

## Running Each Interview

1. **Ask the core question as written**, then use the card's neutral follow-up probes. Don't rewrite the question live — inconsistent phrasing across candidates weakens comparability.
2. **Use neutral, non-leading follow-ups.** The goal is to understand the candidate's own contribution, reasoning, evidence, and learning — not to hint at the answer you want.
3. **Probe for specificity** when an answer is vague: "What did you specifically do?" "What did you decide, and why?" "What happened as a result?" "What would you do differently?"
4. **Watch the card's warning signs** as you listen, but don't treat one warning sign as disqualifying on its own — weigh it alongside the full answer.
5. **Leave room for silence.** Some of the strongest evidence comes after a pause; don't rush to fill it or rescue the candidate from a hard question.

## After Each Interview

1. **Record evidence before scoring.** Write down what the candidate actually said (quote or close paraphrase) for each competency the question targeted, before assigning a number.
2. **Score against the shared rubric**, not against your impression of the candidate overall. See [scoring-rubric.md](scoring-rubric.md).
3. **Submit scores independently** before any debrief discussion, so later interviewers' scores aren't anchored by earlier ones.

## The Debrief

1. **Compare evidence, not just scores.** When scores diverge, share the specific evidence behind each — this surfaces genuine disagreement about the candidate versus miscalibration between interviewers.
2. **Decide against the role's priority competencies**, not a generic bar. A candidate strong on customer understanding but weaker on technical delivery may be exactly right for one role and wrong for another.
3. **Document the reasoning**, not only the outcome, so the decision is defensible and so future loops for similar roles can learn from it.

## Scope And Limits

This guide structures the conversation; it does not replace interviewer training, legal review of your hiring process, or human judgment about a specific candidate and role. See the [README](../README.md#scope) for the project's full scope statement.
