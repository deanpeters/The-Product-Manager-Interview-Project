# When Growth Could Harm Customers

> Tell me about a product decision where increasing engagement, conversion, or retention could also have made the customer's life worse.

> **Follow-up** A design change would increase a key engagement metric by 15%, but a subset of customers appears to be using the product compulsively or misunderstanding what it's doing. What do you do?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Ethics, Self-Awareness, And Responsible Decision-Making
- Metrics, Outcomes, And Learning

## Why This Matters

Metrics that look purely positive on a dashboard can still represent harm to users — compulsive use, dark patterns, exploited defaults. This question directly tests whether a candidate weighs user trust and wellbeing alongside business metrics, and whether they'll act on that judgment when it costs the business something measurable.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- How did you first notice the tension between the metric and the customer's actual interest?
- What did you do about it, and what did it cost in terms of the metric or the business case?
- In the follow-up scenario, how would you actually investigate whether it's a real problem before deciding?
- Who else needed to be part of that decision?

## Strong Evidence

1. Identifies a real tension between a business metric and customer wellbeing from their own experience, not a hypothetical.
2. Describes a concrete action taken — the metric was resisted, redesigned, or the launch was slowed — even at a real cost.
3. In the scenario follow-up, proposes a way to actually investigate the harm (e.g., talk to affected users, look at usage patterns) before either shipping or killing the change outright.
4. Shows awareness that this isn't a solo decision — names who else should weigh in (legal, research, leadership).

## Warning Signs

1. Cannot identify a real tension between metrics and customer interest — treats growth metrics as inherently good.
2. In the scenario, either ships without investigating or kills the idea without investigating — no evidence of wanting to actually understand the harm first.
3. Frames the issue purely as a PR or legal risk rather than a genuine concern for the customer.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Real example of a metric/wellbeing tension with a costly action taken, and a thoughtful, investigative response to the scenario.
- **3:** Recognizes the tension conceptually but the personal example or the scenario response is thin.
- **1:** Treats engagement growth as unambiguously good, or gives a reflexive answer without real investigation.
