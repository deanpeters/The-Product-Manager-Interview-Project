# The Lever the CFO Can See

> An AI feature you shipped has impressive engagement numbers — usage is up, people keep coming back to it. But nobody can draw a clean line from that engagement to revenue, cost savings, or any other number a CFO would recognize. Walk me through how you'd build the business case — or decide there isn't one.

> **Follow-up** Suppose the honest answer is that the case isn't there yet. What do you do with the feature?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization

## AI Competency

[AI Economics & Technical Tradeoffs](../../docs/ai-competency-model.md#ai-economics--technical-tradeoffs)

## Why This Matters

Only use this card for roles where AI is a real part of the product surface — see [ai-competency-model.md](../../docs/ai-competency-model.md). AI features generate their own kind of vanity metric: engagement that feels meaningful because the interaction is novel, without ever translating into a lever finance actually tracks. "Build / Borrow / Buy" tests reasoning about sourcing and technical tradeoffs; this card tests something different — whether a candidate can translate AI value into one specific, defensible economic lever (revenue, cost, retention, compliance risk avoided) rather than defending a feature on engagement alone. A candidate who can't make this translation, or who inflates a soft signal into a hard one, is showing exactly the gap that gets AI investment cut in a budget review.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What specific lever would you try to connect the feature to first, and why that one?
- What evidence would you need to make that connection credible, rather than aspirational?
- How would you talk about this with someone who doesn't trust "engagement" as a metric at all?
- If the connection genuinely isn't there, how do you decide whether the feature is worth keeping anyway?

## Strong Evidence

1. Names one specific, finance-legible lever (not a vague "it drives value") and explains why that lever is the right one for this feature.
2. Describes real evidence they'd gather to support the connection — a controlled comparison, a cohort analysis, a cost-avoidance calculation — not just a correlation between engagement and a metric that moved for other reasons.
3. Can speak the language a skeptical finance stakeholder would actually respond to, translating product terms into business terms.
4. In the follow-up, gives an honest answer when the case isn't there — sunsetting, re-scoping, or explicitly funding it as a bet rather than pretending the case exists.

## Warning Signs

1. Defends the feature on engagement alone, treating usage as self-evidently valuable.
2. No real plan to gather evidence connecting the feature to a finance-legible outcome — just assertion.
3. In the follow-up, can't accept that the case might not exist — keeps arguing rather than making a real call.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Names a specific, well-reasoned economic lever, describes credible evidence to support it, speaks finance's language convincingly, and gives an honest answer when the case doesn't hold up.
- **3:** Names a plausible lever but the evidence plan is thin, or wavers when asked what happens if the case isn't there.
- **1:** Defends engagement as inherently valuable with no translation to an economic lever finance would recognize.
