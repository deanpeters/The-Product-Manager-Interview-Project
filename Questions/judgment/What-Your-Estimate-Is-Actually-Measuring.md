# What Your Estimate Is Actually Measuring

> A sizing rubric like this feeds a single point value or week estimate from several separate inputs: architectural impact, whether the work touches new technology, similarity to past work, team capacity, multi-team dependencies. Two completely different tickets can land on the exact same number for completely different reasons. Walk me through what you think that number is actually measuring, and what gets lost when it's compressed to one value.

> **Follow-up** If you had to keep only one of those input dimensions to protect against the single riskiest kind of surprise, which would you keep, and why?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization
- Delivery, Execution, And Ownership

## Why This Matters

Most sizing frameworks look like they measure effort — time, lines of code, "how much work." Look at the actual inputs and they're almost entirely proxies for what a team *doesn't know yet*: technical unknowns (architectural impact, new technology), familiarity unknowns (similarity to past work), and coordination unknowns (multi-team dependencies, available capacity). This card tests whether a candidate sees past the number to what it's actually a stand-in for, and understands that collapsing several distinct kinds of risk into one scalar throws away the information a team would need to actually mitigate that risk.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- Can you give an example of two pieces of work that would land on the same size but for genuinely different reasons?
- What would you actually do differently if you knew which specific dimension was driving a high number?
- Is there a dimension here you think doesn't belong — something that isn't really about risk at all?

## Strong Evidence

1. Identifies that the rubric's inputs are proxies for uncertainty and risk, not direct measures of effort or work volume.
2. Gives a concrete example of two items landing on the same number for different underlying reasons.
3. Explains that a single compressed number loses the information needed to choose the right mitigation — a spike or prototype for technical risk looks nothing like a stakeholder-alignment plan for coordination risk.
4. Gives real reasoning for which dimension they'd protect, tied to which kind of surprise is costliest to discover late, not just picking one arbitrarily.

## Warning Signs

1. Describes the number as a direct measure of effort or time with no mention of risk or uncertainty.
2. Can't produce a real example of two same-sized-but-different-reason items.
3. No real reasoning for the follow-up's dimension choice — just picks one without justification.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Clearly identifies the rubric as a risk proxy, gives a real example of information loss from compression, and gives well-reasoned prioritization in the follow-up.
- **3:** Some recognition that the number represents more than pure effort, but the reasoning stays generic.
- **1:** Treats the number as a direct measure of work volume with no deeper read on what it represents.
