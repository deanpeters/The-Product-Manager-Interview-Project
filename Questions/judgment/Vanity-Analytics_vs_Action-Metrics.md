# Vanity Metrics vs. Actionable Analytics

> Could you provide some examples of where you encountered vanity metrics?

> **Follow-up** How would you compare these to instances where you've used actionable analytics instead?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Metrics, Outcomes, And Learning
- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization

## Why This Matters

Metrics that look impressive but don't tell you what to do next — raw page views, follower counts, total downloads — can quietly steer a team toward the wrong priorities and create false confidence. A PM who can't distinguish vanity metrics from actionable ones will struggle to set goals, evaluate experiments, or push back when a metric is being used to avoid a hard conversation about outcomes.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What made you realize a metric you were using was vanity rather than actionable?
- What did you replace it with, and how did that change what the team did?
- Was there ever a moment you had to argue against a favorite vanity metric someone else on the team or in leadership was attached to?

## Strong Evidence

1. Cites specific, real examples of vanity metrics they've encountered — e.g., raw page views, total registered followers, aggregate search volume — with enough detail to show they actually worked with these numbers, not just recognize the category.
2. Articulates what makes a metric actionable: it should tell the team what action to take to move toward a goal (e.g., A/B test results, conversion rate) rather than just describing scale.
3. Can explain why an actionable metric needs to be accessible (understandable across technical and non-technical audiences) and auditable (traceable back to source data that others can verify).
4. Describes a real instance of switching a team or project from a vanity metric to an actionable one, including what changed as a result.

## Warning Signs

1. Can name vanity metrics but cannot explain what makes an alternative "actionable" beyond "it's more useful."
2. Only gives textbook examples with no evidence of personal experience distinguishing the two in practice.
3. Treats vanity metrics uncritically as a legitimate goal on their own (e.g., "we wanted to grow followers") without recognizing the trap.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Specific real examples of both vanity and actionable metrics from their own work, clear articulation of what makes a metric actionable, and a concrete story of shifting a team's focus as a result.
- **3:** Can define the difference correctly and give plausible examples, but leans more on textbook knowledge than a specific personal example.
- **1:** Cannot articulate a meaningful distinction, or treats vanity metrics as unproblematic goals.
