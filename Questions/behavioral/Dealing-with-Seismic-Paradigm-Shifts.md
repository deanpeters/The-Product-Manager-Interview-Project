# Dealing with Seismic Paradigm Shifts

> Tell me about a situation in which you had to adjust quickly to a significant change in the products you manage and/or the teams you serve — because of a seismic shift in organization, department, or team priorities. How did the change affect you, and what did you do?

## Type

Behavioral

## Competencies Assessed

- Delivery, Execution, And Ownership
- Ethics, Self-Awareness, And Responsible Decision-Making

## Why This Matters

Economic, political, and organizational events routinely upend a product's priorities with little warning — a reorg, a leadership change, an acquisition, a sudden strategy pivot. A strong PM maintains effectiveness through that disruption instead of freezing or checking out. How a candidate narrates the change — with ownership and learning, or with blame — is as revealing as what they actually did.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What was actually driving the change, as best you understood it at the time?
- How did you feel about it initially, and did that change?
- What specifically did you do differently in the weeks after the shift?
- Looking back, is there anything about how you handled it you'd change?

## Strong Evidence

1. Explains the underlying cause of the shift, not just the surface event — a layoff from missed targets is a different situation than an acquisition by private equity, and the candidate treats them differently.
2. Treats the change as an opportunity for learning or growth rather than something purely imposed on them; speaks about it without bitterness or blame toward leadership or the team.
3. Describes specific, decisive actions taken to keep the product and team functioning through the transition.
4. Names concrete changes they made to workflow, process, requirements, team structure, or how they worked with the team's culture as a result.

## Warning Signs

1. Describes only the change itself, with no account of what the candidate actually did in response.
2. Expresses ongoing bitterness or blame toward the organization, leadership, or teammates rather than reflection.
3. Frames themselves as a passive recipient of the change rather than someone who took action within it.
4. Cannot name a specific action or adjustment — the answer stays at the level of feelings about the change.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Names the real underlying cause, shows a clear shift from initial reaction to constructive action, describes specific decisive steps taken, and reflects on what changed in how they worked afterward — without blame.
- **3:** A real change and some response are described, but the account is thinner — vague on specific actions, or lingering frustration comes through without much self-reflection.
- **1:** No real example, or the account is dominated by blame and passivity with no visible action taken.
