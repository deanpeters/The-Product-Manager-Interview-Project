# Building Trust with New Teams

> Tell me about a time you took over as product owner or product manager for an established, already-formed team. How did you go about building trust with them?

> **Follow-up #1** Now compare that with a time you joined or stood up a newly formed team. What was different about earning trust there?

> **Follow-up #2** Was there a team member who was particularly resistant to you? How did you handle that relationship?

## Type

Behavioral

## Competencies Assessed

- Influence, Collaboration, And Communication
- Ethics, Self-Awareness, And Responsible Decision-Making

## Why This Matters

PMs lead teams they usually don't manage. Trust is the mechanism that makes that work, and it has to be earned differently depending on whether a team already has its own history and norms or is forming for the first time. This question also surfaces how a candidate handles a colleague who doesn't warm to them immediately — a near-universal situation that reveals a lot about self-awareness and respect for others.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What did you specifically do in your first few weeks with the established team?
- How did you know trust was building — what changed in how the team worked with you?
- What was genuinely different about building trust with people who had no prior working relationship with each other, either?
- Looking back, is there anything you'd do differently?

## Strong Evidence

1. Goes beyond a surface description ("I listened, I communicated") and names specific actions taken and why, ideally from a servant-leadership vantage point.
2. Clearly distinguishes the two situations — established team vs. newly formed team — rather than giving the same generic answer to both.
3. Describes the resistant-team-member situation without making it personal: treats the person with dignity and fairness rather than framing them as an obstacle or villain.
4. Demonstrates active listening and a willingness to incorporate the team's own ideas and objections, not just present a plan and defend it.
5. May cite concrete mechanisms — retrospectives, 1:1s, shadowing the team's existing rituals before introducing new ones — used specifically to build trust.

## Warning Signs

1. Answer stays abstract ("communication is key," "I always build trust") with no specific example or action.
2. Describes the resistant team member in a way that blames or diminishes them rather than reflecting on the relationship.
3. Gives essentially the same answer for the established-team and newly-formed-team follow-ups, suggesting no real reflection on what's different about each.
4. No sign of listening to or incorporating the team's perspective — trust-building framed as something done *to* the team rather than *with* them.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Specific story for both situations, concrete actions with reasoning, a resistant-teammate account handled with visible respect and fairness, and evidence the candidate adjusted their approach based on what the team needed.
- **3:** A real example exists, but it's thinner — general actions without much reasoning, or the two situations aren't clearly distinguished, or the resistant-teammate account is vague.
- **1:** No real example, only platitudes, or the account of a difficult teammate reveals blame rather than reflection.

## Role Or Level Notes

For a Director/VP-level candidate, the interesting version of this question is trust across teams they don't sit inside day-to-day, not just one team. Add a follow-up: "How did you build trust with a team's manager or lead, separately from the team itself?" — a candidate operating at that altitude should have a distinct answer for peer-leader trust, not just individual-contributor trust.
