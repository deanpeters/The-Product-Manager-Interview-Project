# Negotiating the Technical-Debt Fire

> Engineering is telling you a pile of unmanaged technical debt is a ticking time bomb. Stakeholders want new features shipped on schedule. Walk me through how you'd actually resolve this — not just how you'd balance it in your head.

> **Follow-up** How would you explain the risk to a stakeholder with no technical background, without getting lost in the weeds?

## Type

Situational

## Competencies Assessed

- Delivery, Execution, And Ownership
- Influence, Collaboration, And Communication

## Why This Matters

Technical debt competes with new features for the same limited capacity, and it's invisible to most stakeholders until it causes real damage. This question tests whether a candidate has a real, systemic way of resolving that tension — not just a one-time argument — and whether they can make the risk legible to someone without a technical background.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- How do you decide how much capacity goes to debt versus features, on an ongoing basis?
- What real-world example or analogy would you use to explain the risk?
- How would you contain the blast radius if something did go wrong?

## Strong Evidence

1. Builds debt paydown into the normal workflow — a recurring allocation, not a one-off special ask that has to be re-won every cycle.
2. Explains the risk using a concrete, real-world framing rather than technical jargon a non-technical stakeholder can't act on.
3. Describes a way of containing risk when shipping something uncertain — a staged or segmented rollout, a reversible release — rather than an all-or-nothing deployment.
4. Can point to a real or realistic consequence to make the stakes concrete, not just an abstract warning.

## Warning Signs

1. Purely technical answer with no stakeholder communication plan.
2. Treats technical debt as engineering's problem alone, with no PM ownership of the trade-off.
3. No containment strategy for a risky release — "we just ship it and see."

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** A recurring debt-paydown practice, a real plain-language explanation of the risk, and a concrete containment strategy for risky releases.
- **3:** Some awareness of the trade-off, but no recurring practice or the explanation stays technical.
- **1:** No real plan — debt is either ignored or treated as purely engineering's problem.
