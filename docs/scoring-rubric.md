# Scoring Rubric

## Purpose

A shared, evidence-based 1-5 scale so multiple interviewers can score the same answer consistently, without collapsing into a single gut-feel number. Score each competency the question targets independently — see the [competency model](competency-model.md).

## Principles

- Score the evidence given, not the candidate's confidence, polish, or resemblance to you.
- Record the specific evidence *before* assigning a number. If you can't cite what the candidate said, don't score it.
- A candidate can score well on one competency and poorly on another within the same answer. Score each named competency separately rather than producing one blended number.
- "I don't have a direct example" is data, not automatically a low score — judge how the candidate reasons about the hypothetical, and weigh the lack of a direct example accordingly for roles where that experience is expected.
- Do not adjust the scale for candidate seniority. Adjust which questions you ask and how deep you probe instead. A 3 means the same thing across candidates.

## The Scale

### 5 — Exceptional Evidence
Specific, verifiable example with clear reasoning, real trade-offs named, honest account of what didn't work, and demonstrated learning that changed subsequent behavior. Answer would hold up under a skeptical follow-up.

### 4 — Strong Evidence
Specific example with sound reasoning and a real outcome. May lack the depth of self-critique or the breadth of a 5, but the core evidence is solid and the candidate clearly did the thing they're describing.

### 3 — Adequate Evidence
Answer is relevant and plausible but thinner: fewer specifics, reasoning stated but not fully justified, outcome described but not measured, or the candidate leans more on "what we did" than their own contribution. A reasonable baseline hire signal, not a standout.

### 2 — Weak Evidence
Vague, generic, or mostly describes a framework or process rather than what the candidate actually did and decided. Follow-up probes don't surface additional specificity. May over-index on team ("we") with little visible individual reasoning or action.

### 1 — No Credible Evidence
No real example, evasive or contradictory answer, reasoning that doesn't hold up to a basic follow-up, or an answer that reveals a concerning gap for the competency being assessed (e.g., dismissiveness toward customer evidence, no visible ownership of a bad outcome).

## Recording Evidence

For each competency scored, capture:

1. **Quote or close paraphrase** of the key evidence.
2. **The score (1-5)** for that competency.
3. **One line of rationale** tying the score to the scale above — not just "good answer."

This record is what makes cross-interviewer calibration possible. A number with no evidence attached is not useful in a hiring debrief.

## Calibration

- New interviewers should shadow-score a live or recorded interview against an experienced interviewer and compare before scoring solo.
- If two interviewers diverge by more than one point on the same competency from the same answer, discuss the specific evidence each weighted — this is a calibration signal, not a candidate problem.
- Revisit and refine anchors (the descriptions above) if interviewers consistently disagree on the same question; the question or anchor may be ambiguous, not the candidates.

## Worked Examples

These are illustrative constructed examples, not real candidate transcripts. They exist to give calibrating interviewers a concrete reference point against two cards from the [core interview loop](core-interview-loop.md), rather than only the abstract anchors above.

### [Deciding Before You're Certain](../Questions/judgment/Deciding-Before-Youre-Certain.md) (Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization)

- **Score 5 example:** "We had to decide whether to launch in a new region before we had reliable localization data. I knew we were missing X and Y, but waiting eight more weeks to get it would have cost us the seasonal window entirely. I decided we had enough signal from the two comparable markets we'd already entered, and structured the launch as a small, reversible pilot in one city first, specifically so a wrong call wouldn't be expensive. In hindsight, that was close to the right call — we caught a pricing issue in the pilot city before it hit the full region." — Names the missing information, gives explicit reasoning for the timing (seasonal cost of waiting), names a specific risk mitigation (small reversible pilot), and reflects honestly on the outcome.
- **Score 2 example:** "We didn't have all the data we wanted, so I just went with my gut and we launched. It mostly worked out." — A real decision exists but there's no visible reasoning about the trade-off, no named mitigation, and "mostly worked out" isn't a real reflection.

### [A Decision You Got Wrong](../Questions/behavioral/A-Decision-You-Got-Wrong.md) (Ethics, Self-Awareness, And Responsible Decision-Making)

- **Score 5 example:** "I pushed hard for a subscription-tier redesign because I believed price was our main churn driver. It wasn't — the real driver was onboarding confusion, which I'd have found if I'd talked to more churned users before proposing the fix. We spent six weeks on pricing changes that didn't move churn at all. Now I don't let myself propose a fix until I've personally read or heard from at least a handful of the affected users, not just looked at the dashboard." — Owns a specific decision, names the reasoning error, states a real cost, and describes a durable change to their own practice.
- **Score 2 example:** "Early in my career the team shipped a feature that didn't do well, and looking back we probably should have tested it more." — The mistake is described at a team level with the candidate at a safe distance, no personal reasoning error is named, and no durable change in practice is described.

Use these as a reference point when your own notes feel like they're landing "somewhere in the middle" — compare the specificity and ownership in your candidate's answer against the 5 and the 2 above, not just the one-line anchor descriptions.
