# Negotiation Signals

## Purpose

A shared vocabulary for a specific kind of strong evidence: negotiation instinct. Several cards in this toolkit put a candidate in a real stakeholder-pressure scenario — an executive with a strong opinion, a salesperson chasing one deal, a politically dangerous disagreement. This document names the techniques a genuinely skilled negotiator tends to demonstrate *without being asked to*, so interviewers have a concrete vocabulary for what "strong evidence" looks like in those cards, beyond the generic Strong Evidence bullets already on each card.

**This is a listening aid, not a quiz.** Don't ask a candidate to name or explain these techniques — that tests memorized vocabulary, exactly what this project avoids. Listen for the candidate spontaneously *doing* one of these things while answering a stakeholder-pressure question, and treat it as a positive signal when they do.

## From Chris Voss — Tactical Empathy Under Pressure

Adapted from *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It* (Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, Harper Business, 2016). See [the author's own page for the book](https://www.blackswanltd.com/never-split-the-difference).

- **Labeling** — naming the other side's emotion or position out loud ("It sounds like you're worried this will slip the deadline") before responding to it. A candidate who does this is de-escalating before problem-solving, not skipping straight to defense.
- **Mirroring** — reflecting back the last few words or the core of what someone said, prompting them to keep explaining rather than the candidate filling the silence with their own argument.
- **Calibrated questions** — open "How" and "What" questions ("How would we know if this worked?" "What makes this the right time?") instead of closed yes/no questions, used to get the other party to help solve the problem rather than simply defend a position.
- **The "that's right" moment** — summarizing the other side's position so accurately that they respond "that's right" (real recognition) rather than "you're right" (a brush-off to end the conversation). A candidate describing this distinction shows they're tracking real understanding, not just agreement.
- **Accusation audit** — naming the worst assumptions the other side might be making about you, out loud, before they have to ("You probably think I'm just trying to protect my own roadmap here") — defusing an objection before it's raised.

## From William Ury — Principled Negotiation

Adapted from *Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In* (Roger Fisher, William Ury, and Bruce Patton) and *Getting Past No: Negotiating with Difficult People* (William Ury). See [the author's own pages for these books](https://www.williamury.com/getting-to-yes/) ([Getting Past No](https://www.williamury.com/getting-past-no/)).

- **Separating people from the problem** — a candidate who addresses the disagreement itself without characterizing the other person as the obstacle.
- **Interests, not positions** — digging into *why* someone wants what they're asking for, rather than treating their stated request as fixed and non-negotiable.
- **Objective criteria** — resolving a disagreement by appeal to some standard both sides can accept (data, an agreed process, a precedent) rather than pure willpower or authority.
- **BATNA (Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement)** — a candidate who knows and can name their real fallback if this particular negotiation fails, and reasons from that fallback rather than treating the current ask as the only option.
- **Going to the balcony** — a candidate who describes deliberately stepping back from a reactive, in-the-moment response to think clearly, rather than reacting immediately to pressure or provocation.
- **Building a golden bridge** — making it easy, and face-saving, for the other party to change their mind or accept a different outcome — the opposite of forcing someone into a public reversal.

## What This Isn't

Not every strong answer will show one of these signals, and their absence isn't automatically a warning sign — a candidate can handle stakeholder pressure well without using textbook technique. This document sharpens what to notice when it's there; it doesn't replace the card's own Strong Evidence and Warning Signs sections, and it should never become something asked of a candidate directly.
