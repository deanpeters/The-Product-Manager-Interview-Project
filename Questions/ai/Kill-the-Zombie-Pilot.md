# Kill the Zombie Pilot

> You inherit a portfolio of six AI initiatives. Every status report says "green." Walk me through how you'd figure out which ones are actually proven — genuinely used, genuinely working — versus which ones have just accumulated activity without ever being validated.

> **Follow-up** Suppose you find one that's clearly a zombie: lots of meetings, a demo everyone liked, no real usage. How do you actually make the call to stop it stick, against the sunk-cost pressure of everyone who's already invested in it?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization

## AI Competency

[AI Product Operations & Learning](../../docs/ai-competency-model.md#ai-product-operations--learning)

## Why This Matters

Only use this card for roles where AI is a real part of the product surface — see [ai-competency-model.md](../../docs/ai-competency-model.md). AI initiatives are unusually prone to becoming zombies — bets that keep getting funded and staffed because they've generated activity (meetings, demos, decks) without ever being pushed through a real proof bar (talked about → documented → actually used → actually works). "Turning Usage Into a Better Model" tests the feedback loop for a shipped, working AI product; this card tests something upstream and organizational — the discipline of telling a proven bet from an unproven one across a whole portfolio, and the harder skill of making a stop decision stick once sunk cost and social investment are working against it.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What specific evidence would move an initiative from "green" to "actually validated," in your view?
- Who typically resists a stop decision, and why?
- How do you make sure the ones you keep funding are kept for real reasons, not just because they haven't been challenged yet?
- Have you ever been wrong about which bucket an initiative belonged in? What did you learn?

## Strong Evidence

1. Proposes a concrete proof bar distinguishing real validation from mere activity — usage data, a specific outcome metric, or a defined "done" state — rather than accepting self-reported status.
2. Names who tends to resist a stop decision and why (the team that built it, the executive who championed it, the customer who was promised it) and has a real plan for that conversation.
3. Describes making a stop decision stick in practice — a specific mechanism (a documented decision, a reallocated budget line, a communicated rationale) rather than just "I'd tell them to stop."
4. Shows self-awareness about a time their own read on an initiative was wrong, and what corrected it.

## Warning Signs

1. No concrete proof bar — "I'd just look at how it's going" with no specific criteria.
2. Never addresses the social/political difficulty of a stop decision — treats it as purely analytical.
3. The "stop" in the follow-up doesn't actually stop anything — the initiative quietly continues under a different name or reduced scope with no real accountability.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Concrete proof bar for distinguishing validated from zombie initiatives, real named resistance and a credible plan to work through it, and a stop decision that has a real, durable mechanism behind it.
- **3:** Reasonable proof bar but thin on how a stop decision would actually be made to stick against resistance.
- **1:** No real criteria for telling proven from unproven, or no credible path to actually stopping a resistant initiative.
