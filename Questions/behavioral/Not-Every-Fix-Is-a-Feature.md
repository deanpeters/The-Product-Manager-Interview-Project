# Not Every Fix Is a Feature

> Tell me about a real problem you or a team you were on solved *without* building a new feature — through pricing, packaging, process, support, or something else instead. Why was that the right lever, and how did you know a feature would have been the wrong tool?

## Type

Behavioral

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization

## Why This Matters

Most PMs default to "build something" as the answer to every problem, because building is the muscle the job trains most and the artifact that's easiest to point to at review time. This card tests whether a candidate's solution space is actually wider than the feature backlog — whether they can recognize when pricing, packaging, a process change, a support-team fix, or a policy change solves the real problem faster, cheaper, or more durably than shipping code. A candidate who has never once *not* built a feature, or who can't explain why the non-feature lever was the right call, is showing a narrower range of judgment than the job actually requires.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What made you consider a non-feature option in the first place, rather than defaulting to a build?
- Who did you need to involve to make that lever work, since it likely wasn't just engineering?
- How did you know the problem was actually solved, given you couldn't rely on your usual product-usage metrics?
- Was there a build-something option on the table too? Why didn't you take it?

## Strong Evidence

1. Describes a specific, real situation and a specific non-feature lever — not a hypothetical or a generic "we could also change pricing" aside.
2. Explains the reasoning for why that lever fit the problem better than a feature would have — cost, speed, durability, or root-cause fit.
3. Names who else they had to work with to pull the lever (sales, finance, support, legal, ops) and how that collaboration differed from a typical engineering-led build.
4. Describes how they validated the fix worked, using evidence appropriate to that lever rather than forcing it into product-usage metrics.

## Warning Signs

1. Can't produce a real example — every answer drifts back toward a feature they built.
2. The "non-feature" example is really a thin feature in disguise (a config flag, a settings toggle) rather than a genuinely different lever.
3. No reasoning for why the lever fit — just describes what happened with no judgment about why it was the right call.
4. No mention of validating whether the fix actually worked.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Concrete real example, clear reasoning for why the non-feature lever fit the problem better than a build, evidence of cross-functional work beyond engineering, and a real way they confirmed it worked.
- **3:** Real example, plausible reasoning, but thin on either the cross-functional collaboration or the validation.
- **1:** No genuine non-feature example, or the example is a feature relabeled.
