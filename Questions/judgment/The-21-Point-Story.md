# The 21-Point Story

> Your team's sizing guide says never size a story at 21 points — at that scale, you've probably got an epic in disguise. Why would a large number itself be a warning sign, rather than the team just doing the multiplication and moving on?

> **Follow-up** If a story kept coming back at 21 points meeting after meeting, what would you actually do?

## Type

Judgment

## Competencies Assessed

- Product Judgment, Strategy, And Prioritization
- Delivery, Execution, And Ownership

## Why This Matters

A story that lands at 21 points isn't just "a lot of known work" — at that scale, it almost always means the team hasn't actually broken the work down enough to know what's inside it. Assigning it a number anyway creates false precision: a specific-looking figure standing in for something still genuinely unknown. This card tests whether a candidate recognizes that a suspiciously large estimate is a decomposition failure to fix, not a size to accept and schedule.

## Neutral Follow-Up Probes

- What's actually different between a story that's "big but well understood" and one that's "big because we don't know what's in it"?
- How would you tell which of those two situations you're actually in?
- What's the risk of just scheduling the 21-point story as-is and moving on?

## Strong Evidence

1. Explains that a very large estimate usually signals insufficient breakdown or unenumerated unknowns, not simply a large volume of well-understood work.
2. Distinguishes "big and well understood" from "big because we don't actually know what's inside it," and describes a real way to tell them apart.
3. In the follow-up, describes an actual decomposition or discovery step — splitting the story, running a spike, reframing it as its own epic — not just re-estimating the same story again.
4. Names the real risk of scheduling an oversized estimate as-is: hidden scope surfacing mid-sprint, blown timelines, false confidence.

## Warning Signs

1. Treats 21 points as simply "a big task" with no recognition of the hidden-complexity signal.
2. No real distinction between well-understood-big and unknown-big.
3. In the follow-up, just re-estimates or schedules the same story without any real decomposition step.

## Scoring Anchors

- **5:** Clear explanation of the false-precision risk, a real distinction between the two kinds of "big," and a genuine decomposition step in the follow-up.
- **3:** Some recognition that a very large estimate is a signal, but the follow-up lacks a real decomposition action.
- **1:** Treats the large number as just a bigger task to schedule, with no recognition of the underlying risk.
